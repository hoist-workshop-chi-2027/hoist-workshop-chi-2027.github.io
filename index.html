HOIST

Human-Centered Out-of-Hospital Interactive Systems and Technologies for Acute Care

A CHI 2027 Workshop · Pittsburgh, PA · May 10-14, 2027

Overview

Out-of-hospital acute care is a demanding domain for interactive systems research, but it is also a common part of everyday life, spanning injuries, exacerbations of chronic illness, and time-critical emergencies. Care may begin with patients, family members, bystanders, or dispatchers, then move through rapidly assembled teams, transport, and handoff to receiving clinicians. New sensing, wearable, communication, and decision-support technologies are expanding what can be detected, shared, and acted upon across this trajectory. Despite this, HCI research often examines particular technologies or settings separately rather than treating the prehospital care trajectory as a coherent interaction domain. This cross-disciplinary workshop will bring together researchers and clinicians to develop a shared agenda for human-centered systems supporting time-critical care from detection and activation through scene care, transport, and handoff. We explicitly welcome participants without prior acute-care experience, including researchers in adjacent health domains interested in collaboration across informal and formal care stakeholders.

Call for Participation

Prehospital acute care is an unusually demanding setting for interactive systems: care unfolds in public spaces, amid uncertain information and dynamic teams, and marks a transition from everyday life to specialized, time-critical care. Nonetheless, technology and interventions in the first moments of illness can have outsized impact well before a patient reaches the hospital. Building on Pittsburgh’s history of prehospital innovation, this cross-disciplinary workshop will bring together HCI and acute care researchers to build a shared research agenda for prehospital interactive systems. We welcome researchers, clinicians, practitioners, designers, and others interested in the topic, including those without prior experience in emergency care and those working on related problems involving informal and formal health stakeholders. Participants may submit a 1-2 page position paper, system paper, or prototype description presenting relevant work, deployments, methodological experience, or open problems. Submissions will be collected through a form linked from the workshop website. Submissions will be reviewed for relevance and diversity of perspective, with accepted authors receiving priority seating. The workshop will remain open to all registered CHI attendees, and we expect approximately 20 participants. During two consecutive sessions, participants will map challenges across detection and activation, scene care, transport, and handoff, then use scenario-based design activities to identify research questions, technical opportunities, collaborations, and evaluation methods. With author permission, accepted submissions will be collected in an arXiv workshop proceedings volume. At least two organizers will register for CHI 2027 and attend the workshop in person.

Motivation

Prehospital medicine is a distinct yet familiar setting for human-computer interaction because care takes place close to patients, outside controlled clinical environments, and across the spaces of everyday life. An emergency may first become visible to a wearable sensor, a family member, a passerby, or a 9-1-1 dispatcher. If severe, care then moves through rapidly assembled teams at the scene (first informal, then formal), extrication or rescue, personal vehicle or medical transport, and a compressed transfer of information and responsibility to a receiving care team. Each transition brings a different environment: the people, the formality of roles, information available, physical environment, available devices, and consequences of interaction failure. These settings combine many of the conditions that HCI studies separately: time pressure; uncertainty and incomplete information; hands-busy and eyes-busy work; personal information management and the collision of public and private spaces and data; technology-enabled distributed and ad hoc teams; and high costs of false alarms, distraction, or automation failure. Prior HCI and health informatics work has demonstrated the importance of understanding fieldwork in EMS, communication and handoff, trauma-team coordination, and the design of alerts and wearable or head-worn interfaces [1,5-8]. Recent work also points toward rapidly changing operational and clinical practices, including AI integration across EMS workflows [2] and automated detection of emergencies through consumer wearables [3]. Community response systems (e.g. PulsePoint) further extend the paradigm beyond formal responders by recruiting and guiding lay responders before professional crews arrive [4]. Interactive systems across the care trajectory may sense events, recruit the right people, present just-in-time guidance, support team cognition, degrade safely when connectivity or sensing fails, preserve provenance under uncertainty, and hand information forward to the next team. These are fundamental interaction and sociotechnical design problems as much as clinical or algorithmic ones.

ACM and CHI have a long and growing history of interactive health research, including workshops such as WISH (Workshop on Interactive Systems in Healthcare), CHI 2025’s “Envisioning the Future of Interactive Health,” and the recent inaugural ACM Interactive Health conference. This workshop focuses that broader interactive-health agenda on the prehospital care trajectory, bringing together HCI and health researchers working on sensing, AI, mobile and wearable interaction, CSCW, visualization, ubiquitous computing, telemedicine, and other areas relevant to high-acuity field care. By doing so, we hope to form a bridge between the emerging community of interactive health researchers and acute care, emergency response, public health and public-safety research.

Workshop goals

The workshop has four concrete goals:

  • Define a shared design space for prehospital interactive systems across the full care trajectory from emergency detection and activation to hospital handoff.
  • Identify recurring interaction constraints and design tensions that cut across stages of care, as well as problems that are specific to particular stages, roles, or environments.
  • Develop methodological guidance for studying and evaluating systems in infrequent, time-critical, and operationally constrained settings.
  • Build a cross-disciplinary community and a prioritized research agenda that can guide future HCI (e.g., CHI, CSCW, UIST, UbiComp), health informatics (e.g., AMIA), emergency medicine (e.g., NAEMSP), and other health technology collaborations. Importantly, we want to invite anyone interested in this topic to participate, including those without prior experience in acute care. We explicitly welcome researchers working in longitudinal health, chronic care, and other adjacent domains who are interested in contributing.

A care trajectory framework

We will organize discussion around four linked stages. These stages are not intended as a rigid clinical model; they are a workshop scaffold for locating interaction problems and following information, responsibility, and technology as care moves.

  • Detection & Activationrecognizing an emergency; passive sensing and wearables; bystander action; 9-1-1 and dispatch; responder recruitment; public-access guidance; and deciding when a computational or sensing system should escalate.
  • Scene, Resuscitation & Rescueassessment and triage; acute resuscitation; procedural and decision support; shared situation awareness; documentation; extrication; interaction under noise, motion, stress, gloves, and divided attention; and coordination among rapidly assembled teams.
  • Transport & Sustainmentpersonal vehicle, ambulance, and air-medical care; continuous monitoring and telemedicine for austere or distant settings; reassessment and deterioration; route and destination decisions; constrained space; and maintaining useful assistance over an evolving episode of care.
  • Handoff & Continuitypre-arrival communication; transfer of patient state, interventions, uncertainty, and rationale; transition from EMS to definitive care, emergency, or trauma teams; reconciling records; preserving provenance; and preventing information loss as responsibility changes.

Across all four stages, we will examine cross-cutting questions about sensing and AI, multimodal and wearable interaction, teamwork and CSCW, resilient support, interoperability, explainability and uncertainty, workflow integration, ecological evaluation, and deployment.

Guiding questions

  • What interaction constraints recur across the prehospital trajectory, and which demands are unique to particular stages of care?
  • How should sensing, interpretation, decision authority, and action be distributed among patients, bystanders, dispatchers, EMS clinicians, receiving teams, and computational agents?
  • How should prehospital systems communicate uncertainty and fail or degrade safely when sensing, models, networks, or human attention are unreliable?
  • How can technology support shared situation awareness without adding alert burden, documentation work, or coordination overhead?
  • What information should persist across transitions, and how should interactive systems support continuity from scene observations and interventions to hospital decision-making?
  • What research methods, simulations, datasets, field studies, and outcome measures can produce ecologically valid evidence while respecting the clinical and operational constraints of emergency care?
  • What opportunities for collaboration with existing care systems or applications are there?

Activities

The workshop runs as two consecutive 90-minute sessions separated by the conference break. The design intentionally limits conventional presentations: invited experts and participants provide short inputs, while most workshop time is devoted to structured collaborative work that produces shared artifacts.

Pre-workshop participation and preparation

Since CHI 2027 workshops are open to conference attendees, we will use submissions to seed the workshop. We will invite 1-2 page position or system papers describing a real workflow, deployment, design problem, prototype or open technical question. We will explicitly welcome early-stage work, deployed systems, methodological reflections, and clinically grounded problems in addition to conventional research submissions. Accepted-submission authors will receive priority seating according to CHI 2027 procedures; remaining seats will be open to other registered CHI attendees.

Before the workshop, organizers will group submissions by technologies, care stages, stakeholders, and recurring challenges to help guide the workshop. Participants will receive the accepted materials and a one-page orientation to the four-stage framework two weeks prior to the workshop. Authors may optionally contribute one slide, image, or system artifact for a shared case gallery; there will not be a sequence of full paper presentations.

Session 1 - Mapping the landscape of prehospital interaction

0-10 minWelcome, framing, rapid participant introductions, and explanation of the care trajectory framework and workshop outputs.
10-40 minThree or four 5-minute provocations spanning complementary clinical, HCI, sensing, and deployed-system perspectives—for example, automated detection and community activation; field resuscitation and clinical decision support; coordination and handoff; and a deployed public-access or EMS technology. Provocateurs will use their time to pose concrete design and operational tensions rather than deliver keynotes.
40-60 minCare trajectory mapping in four mixed groups (Detection & Activation; Scene, Resuscitation & Rescue; Transport & Sustainment; Handoff & Continuity). Groups map actors, information flows, devices/interfaces, decisions, breakdowns, environmental constraints, and unresolved technical opportunities.
60-80 minGallery walk (using digital canvas) and cross-stage synthesis. Participants rotate through the maps and annotate tensions that recur across stages, dependencies between stages, and places where optimizing one stage can create problems downstream.
80-90 minWhole room prioritization of the most consequential cross-cutting design tensions and open research questions to carry into Session 2.

Session 2 - Collaboratively synthesizing a research agenda

0-10 minRecap of the stage maps and formation of mixed teams around the prioritized tensions.
10-50 minScenario-based design session. Teams work from realistic high-acuity scenarios such as an unwitnessed cardiac arrest, a motor-vehicle trauma with extrication, deterioration during critical-care transport, or a compressed EMS-to-trauma-team handoff. Each team must specify the intended users, sensing and data inputs, interaction concept, information that moves forward, likely failure modes, safe degradation behavior, and an evaluation strategy.
50-70 minDeployment and methods stress test. Teams exchange concepts and critique them using prompts covering cognitive load, false positives/negatives, connectivity, privacy/consent, interoperability, equity, clinical workflow, model uncertainty, and feasibility of field evaluation.
70-85 minResearch agenda synthesis. Organizers cluster the resulting challenges and methods into research questions, infrastructure needs, and technical opportunities. Participants vote on priorities and identify areas requiring (or enabling) cross-disciplinary collaboration.
85-90 minCommitments and next steps, including ownership of the public synthesis, follow-up community channels, and potential collaborative outputs.

Participants, outputs, and follow-up

We expect approximately 20 participants, with small-group activities designed for 4-6 people per group, each with a facilitator.

The workshop will produce: (1) a care trajectory map of interactive system opportunities and constraints; (2) a set of cross-cutting design tensions and failure modes; (3) a prioritized research agenda and methodological challenges; and (4) a community of researchers and practitioners interested in continued collaboration. With author permission, accepted submissions will be collected in a publicly accessible arXiv workshop proceedings volume and linked from the workshop website; authors may develop these contributions for later peer-reviewed publication. After CHI, organizers will publish a public workshop synthesis and pursue an appropriate community-facing venue such as ACM Interactions or a follow-on research agenda article. Workshop materials and synthesized artifacts will remain available on the website in accessible digital form.

Accessibility

We will provide workshop materials digitally in accessible formats and design activities so participants can contribute verbally or in writing. We will work with the CHI accessibility team to support additional accommodations requested by participants and adapt workshop materials or participation formats as needed.
